# The One Ring — what Wyrd takes from it

Sourced from *The One Ring* Core Rules (336pp), *The Adventurer's Companion* (160pp) and
*The Darkening of Mirkwood* (144pp). 20 TOR PDFs in the library.

Mechanics summarised for implementation. Not a substitute for the books.

Different setting, same problem. TOR is built for **a long defeat played over decades by
people who cannot win** — which is closer to Wyrd's needs than anything in the WFRP line.
Where WFRP has better corruption *tables*, TOR has a better corruption *model*, and it is
the only system reviewed here that seriously solves the multi-year campaign.

## 1. Hope vs Shadow — corruption as a balance, not a ratchet

Two paired scores:

- **Hope** — spendable, renewable reserve of "spiritual fortitude and positivity". Spend to
  invoke an Attribute bonus after a failed roll (1 point per roll, max). Recovered by
  spending Fellowship points and during downtime.
- **Shadow** — "the marks left on a character when his spirit is tainted by doubt and
  despair." Starts at 0. Accrues and sticks.

**When Hope falls to equal the Shadow rating, the character becomes Miserable.** Note the
direction: it is Hope *descending* to meet Shadow, as much as Shadow rising. A hero with
Shadow 0 never becomes Miserable no matter how spent they are. At Hope 0 they are
*hopeless* — cannot continue any struggle, and will flee any source of danger or stress.

**Why this beats WFRP's model for Wyrd.** WFRP corruption is one-directional: it rises until
you break. TOR makes damnation a *relationship between two numbers*, which means:

- the same Shadow score means different things at different times
- recovery is a real part of the loop, not an afterthought
- a deeply shadowed character can still be functioning because their Hope is high — and
  collapse later without gaining any new Shadow at all

For a chronicle running years, that is a much richer state model, and it gives the GM a
legible dial for "how close to the edge is he *right now*" as distinct from "how far gone
is he *permanently*".

**Corruption tests:** Feat die + Success dice equal to Wisdom, TN normally 14, raised or
lowered by severity. Failure gains 1 Shadow "or more if threat is severe". Triggered by
Anguish or by traversing a Blighted place — so both moral and environmental, as in WFRP.

## 2. Shadow Weakness — your motivation is what damns you

Every hero has a **Calling** (why they adventure) and each Calling carries a matching
**Shadow Weakness** — "the path he would follow if he failed to resist the Shadow's
influence."

- *Slayer* (revenge for a loss) → **Curse of Vengeance**
- *Treasure-hunter* → **Dragon-sickness**

This is excellent and Wyrd should steal it outright. Corruption is not a generic taint
meter; it is **the specific corruption of the specific thing that drives you**. The
rat-catcher who took up the trade to feed his family falls a different way from the
scholar who wanted to know what was in the book.

It also pairs directly with Warlock's **Passions** and **Cruel Misfortune** — those already
establish what a character cares about and what haunts them. Deriving the corruption path
from those means the dark road is authored at character creation, not improvised at the
threshold.

## 3. The Feat die — state-gated special faces

The **Feat die** is a d12 reading 1–10 plus two icons: the **Gandalf rune** and the **Eye of
Sauron**. Rolled on every test alongside a pool of d6 **Success dice**.

Two conditions change how dice read:

- **Weary** (Endurance at or below Fatigue): all Success dice showing 1–3 count as 0.
- **Miserable**: *"When a Miserable hero makes a roll and gets an Eye of Sauron on his Feat
  die, he suffers a bout of madness and temporarily loses control of himself."*

**This is the precedent for Wyrd's dice design.** The Eye is always on the die, but it only
bites when your spiritual state has already gone bad. The consequence is **gated by the
corruption track**, not by the roll.

That is exactly the mechanic proposed for the Wyrd die — the Chaos Star face widening or
sharpening as Corruption rises. TOR did it first, and it validates the shape: a constant
die, a variable threat, driven by state.

Worth being clear on what does *not* transfer: the Feat die's icons **override the
outcome** (auto-success / auto-failure) rather than sitting beside it. So TOR is *not* a
solution to the independent-axes problem — Genesys and the Wrath die remain the references
there. TOR's contribution is the state-gating, not the axes.

## 4. Adventuring Phase / Fellowship Phase — the year as the unit

Play alternates:

- **Adventuring Phase** — the adventure. Loremaster-driven; players react.
- **Fellowship Phase** — downtime. *Player*-driven: "the Loremaster is invited to sit back
  and follow what his players have to say about their characters."

**"The default pacing of gameplay for The One Ring sees a group of adventurers take part in
one adventure per year of game time."** The Fellowship Phase marks the year's end.

Fellowship Phase structure:

| Step | What happens |
|---|---|
| Choose Destination | Repair to a Sanctuary, or the company disbands temporarily and companions go home |
| Standing Upkeep | If away from home, **lose 1 Standing or spend Treasure equal to current Standing** |
| Spend Experience | Wisdom, Valour, Weapon skills |
| Spend Advancement | Common skills |
| Choose Undertaking | One activity (two if a longer stop) — e.g. **Heal Corruption** to reduce Shadow |

**This is the answer to the "long quiet" problem.** I argued Wyrd needs stretches of low
stakes between escalations. TOR doesn't skip them — it makes downtime *a phase with its own
rules, its own costs, and its own player agency*. Standing Upkeep means time itself extracts
a price. Heal Corruption means recovery is something you must *choose to spend a year on*,
at the cost of not doing something else.

For a solo player dipping in from a phone, a Fellowship Phase is also the ideal
low-intensity session: no combat, no danger, pure character, and it advances the calendar.

## 5. The Tale of Years — a thirty-year campaign that actually exists

*The Darkening of Mirkwood* runs **2947–2977**, one adventure per year, grouped into named
eras that tell the whole arc by themselves:

- **The Last Good Years** (2947–2950)
- **The Return of the Shadow** (2951–2960)
- **The Years of the Plague** (2967–2974)
- **The Darkening of Mirkwood** (2975–2977)

Each year is given in a fixed format: **Year**, **Events**, **Fellowship Phase: Year's End**
(special undertakings or circumstances for that year), and an adventure.

### The thesis — read this before designing Wyrd's campaign layer

> "It will be the telling of a long defeat for the Free Folks of the North... Even heroes
> can hardly avert this fate — all the odds are against them. But there is something they
> can certainly do: they can fight to hold back the darkness for another day, another month,
> maybe another year. Their plight may be considered desperate, even hopeless, but they will
> at least save something from the inevitable doom."

And:

> "The emphasis... should be on personal tragedy. The forest may be lost, but can the heroes
> save their own families and friends from slavery and death?... They may not live to see the
> downfall of the Enemy."

This is the low-fantasy escalation model, stated better than I stated it. **What escalates
is the world's darkness, not the heroes' power.** Victory is holding the line one more year.
The scope stays personal while the stakes rise around you.

### It is explicitly not a script

> "This guide is filled with plot elements that are meant to be woven together with the
> deeds of the players, not a rigid script for them to play through."

Loremasters are told to alter, relocate or discard events entirely, with an in-fiction
licence to do so: *"Middle-earth historians can be wrong, rumours are often mistaken for
truth and what is written may not be what actually came to pass."*

For an LLM GM this framing is valuable in itself — canonical permission to deviate, with a
diegetic justification for why the record and the reality differ.

### Events happen whether you attend

> "If the year's main events are in Southern Mirkwood, and the players are currently in the
> Woodland Realm, there is no need to force them to travel the length of Mirkwood — either
> run another adventure, or let them simply hear later about what transpired."

This settles the open question about whether Wyrd's world should advance between sessions.
It should — and the player's absence is normal, not a failure state.

## 6. News from Afar — information latency as a feature

TOR gives explicit travel times for *news*:

- within the region: **1 year**, sometimes more
- from outside the region: **2–5 years**
- from the South, East or West: **3–8 years**

Characters with relevant Lore may hear sooner; powerful figures have their own networks.

> "In Middle-earth, miles are miles, and someone has to cross them to bring word from afar."

**This is the single most useful device here for a solo campaign.** It lets the world move
continuously offstage while guaranteeing the player learns about it *late, partially, and
unreliably* — which is both period-accurate for the Old World and exactly how you want
information to reach one person who cannot be everywhere. It also means the GM can advance
faction clocks freely without owing the player a report.

## 7. Holdings — something to lose

Rules for the players owning and developing a **holding**. The design intent is stated
plainly:

> "They make a character part of the setting — and they are wonderful leverage for the
> Loremaster to use. If Spiders attack a Woodman village, that's a tragedy for the heroes to
> avenge — but if the village includes a farm that is part of a hero's holding, then it's
> personal."

And the dilemma it manufactures:

> "What will a hero choose when faced with the option of remaining faithful to an important
> quest, or return home to protect his own?"

For Wyrd this is the mechanism that converts a long campaign's accumulated investment into
*stakes*. Over years the player builds something; the world then threatens it. No invention
required.

## 8. Kin & Hearth, and generational play

> "For heroes native to Wilderland, danger is not over the Misty Mountains and far away — it
> is right outside their door."

And because thirty years exceeds an adventuring career, TOR expects **succession**: heroes
"get married, settle down, have children, and eventually hand over their weapons to their
sons and daughters." Dwarves and Elves pursue similarly long-termed goals — founding a
settlement, recovering a long-lost heirloom.

Worth holding in reserve for Wyrd. A chronicle genuinely running years may outlast one
rat-catcher, and "your daughter inherits the ledger and the enemies" is a far better
answer to eventual death than a fresh character with no history.

## Summary — what Wyrd takes

| Take | Why |
|---|---|
| **Hope / Shadow duality** | Corruption as a balance with real recovery, not a one-way ratchet |
| **Shadow Weakness from Calling** | Your motivation determines how you fall; pairs with Warlock's Passions |
| **State-gated die face** | The Chaos Star bites harder as Corruption rises — TOR's Eye/Miserable rule |
| **Adventuring / Fellowship phases** | Makes the "long quiet" a real phase with costs and choices |
| **One adventure per year** | The pacing unit for a multi-year chronicle |
| **Tale of Years format** | Year / Events / Year's End / adventure — a directly liftable campaign schema |
| **The long defeat thesis** | The world darkens; you hold the line one more year |
| **News from Afar latency** | The world moves offstage; you hear late, partial, unreliable |
| **Holdings** | Accumulated investment becomes stakes |
| **Succession** | An answer to death that keeps the chronicle's history |

Between TOR and WFRP 3e, the structural layer of Wyrd is now almost entirely sourced from
prior art. What remains genuinely original is running it for **one player with an NPC
party**, and having the GM *select and sequence* scenarios against live threads rather than
following an authored Tale of Years.
